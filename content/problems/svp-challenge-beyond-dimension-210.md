The SVP challenge supplies a random lattice basis in a given dimension d (seed 0). **Task:** submit a lattice vector, given by integer coordinates relative to the published basis, in dimension d ≥ 211 whose Euclidean norm is below the challenge's Gauss-heuristic threshold, thereby exceeding the current record of dimension 210.

# State of the art

The Hall of Fame (October 2026) lists dimension 210, norm 3808, by Jintai Ding and Ziyu Zhao (January 2026, sieving); dimension 200, norm 3723, by Ziyu Zhao and Jintai Ding (March 2025); dimension 192, norm 3595, by Shi Bai, Yuanmi Chen, Jiangxia Ge, Yituo He, Xiangxue Li, Chao Sun and Yu Yu (September 2026); dimension 190, norm 3613, by Yao Sun and Shuai Chang (July 2024); dimension 188, norm 3582, by Leizhang Wang, Lin Wang, Baocang Wang, Geng Wang and Huiwen Jia (March 2025). All listed entries use sieving. Sieving cost grows exponentially in the dimension, so each step of ten dimensions multiplies the needed memory and time by a large factor.

# Applications

Records calibrate the cost models behind the security levels of lattice-based standards such as ML-KEM and ML-DSA. They do not break those schemes, whose parameters correspond to far larger dimensions.

# Resolution criteria

A submission accepted into the SVP challenge Hall of Fame in a dimension of at least 211, with the vector, the seed, the norm and a description of algorithm and hardware published.

# Plain explanation

Picture a grid of points in 210 dimensions, built from a handful of arrows. The task is to find a non-zero grid point that is unusually close to the starting point. Easy in two dimensions, very hard in hundreds.

A research group at TU Darmstadt runs an open contest. They publish a grid in each dimension, and anyone can submit a close point, with the exact numbers needed to check it. The current record is dimension 210, set by Jintai Ding and Ziyu Zhao in January 2026 using a method called sieving.

Why does anyone care? Encryption designed to resist quantum computers relies on such grid problems being hard. The records show the real price of attacking them. The bigger the records, the more careful designers must be about how big their secret keys should be.

The caveat: the grids used in real encryption are much larger. No contest result threatens them today.

# Student explanation

**The objects.** Put $d$ linearly independent vectors as the columns of a matrix $B$. The **lattice** is every point $Bc$ where $c$ is a vector of whole numbers, a slanted grid in $d$ dimensions. The **shortest vector problem** asks for the nonzero lattice point closest to the origin. A two-dimensional example shows why the basis matters: the columns $(1,0)$ and $(7,1)$ generate the ordinary integer grid, but the short vector $(0,1)$ only appears as $(7,1) - 7\,(1,0)$. In hundreds of dimensions such hidden combinations are very hard to find.

**The challenge.** TU Darmstadt publishes, for each dimension $d$, a random lattice basis generated from seed 0. A valid entry is an integer vector $c$ such that $v = Bc$ is nonzero and its length $\|v\| = \sqrt{v_1^2 + \dots + v_d^2}$ is below a threshold set slightly above the **Gauss heuristic**, the length the shortest vector of a random lattice is expected to have. The task here is any such vector in dimension 211 or higher.

**The record.** The Hall of Fame (October 2026) lists dimension 210, length 3808, by Jintai Ding and Ziyu Zhao (January 2026), ahead of dimension 200 (Zhao and Ding, March 2025), 192 (Bai and co-authors, September 2026) and 190 (Sun and Chang, July 2024). All used **sieving**.

**Why each dimension is hard.** Sieving keeps a very large list of lattice vectors and repeatedly subtracts pairs that point in similar directions; the difference is often shorter, and the list gradually fills with short vectors. The list size and the time both grow exponentially with $d$: each additional dimension multiplies the cost by a roughly constant factor, so ten more dimensions cost many times more memory and GPU time. Polynomial-time methods such as LLL run fast but only find vectors far longer than the threshold. Records are therefore set by groups with large GPU clusters and well-engineered software such as the General Sieve Kernel (G6K).

**Why it matters.** Post-quantum standards such as ML-KEM choose their key sizes from estimated attack costs, and these records are the public, checkable data points those estimates are tested against. Deployed parameters correspond to far larger dimensions, so a record does not threaten them.

**How a claim is checked.** The submission gives $c$; a checker computes $Bc$ (one matrix-vector product), its length, and the comparison with the threshold, then the Hall of Fame lists it with the algorithm and hardware.
