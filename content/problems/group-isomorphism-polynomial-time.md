# State of the art

**Known before November 2026.** The generator-enumeration algorithm runs in $N^{\log_2 N+O(1)}$ time (Tarjan, recorded by Miller, STOC 1978). Rosenbaum (2013) improved the exponent by a constant factor, quoted in the 2026 manuscript as $N^{(1/4+o(1))\log N}$. Polynomial-time algorithms were known for abelian groups, groups without abelian normal subgroups, groups with abelian Sylow towers and, in linear time, for most group orders, but not for prime-power orders. Grochow and Qiao (2019) showed that the class-2, exponent-$p$ case is polynomial-time equivalent to tensor isomorphism. Sun (2023) gave $N^{O((\log N)^{5/6})}$ for that case with $p$ odd, and the tensor bound was improved to $q^{\tilde O(n^{1.5})}$ by Ivanyos, Mendoza, Qiao, Sun and Zhang (FOCS 2024), as cited in the manuscript. Babai's 2015 quasipolynomial graph isomorphism algorithm leaves group isomorphism as one bottleneck.

**Reported in November 2026 (unrefereed).** Qiao and Zhang (manuscript dated 3 November 2026, 282 pages, file 1723 in a GitHub release labelled as STOC 2027 submissions whose hosting organization is not verified, not on arXiv as far as we found) report a deterministic algorithm deciding group isomorphism in time polynomial in $N$, via tensor isomorphism over $\mathbb F_q$ in $q^{O(n)}$ time, the Luks-Miyazaki matrix group divide-and-conquer framework, linearly primitive groups, and group extensions and cohomology. The abstract says its main results were generated by ChatGPT during sustained conversations with the authors, who rewrote them and take full responsibility. A 10-page preprint of 13 November (arXiv:2611.18084) uses the algorithm to claim polynomial-time isomorphism of finite associative rings; it assumes the result and is not independent verification. The manuscript does not claim graph isomorphism is in P.

**Not known either way.** Whether the manuscript's argument is correct (no referee report, independent re-derivation or formalization is recorded), the exact exponent, whether tensor isomorphism over $\mathbb F_q$ is in $q^{o(n)}$, and the status of tournament isomorphism, the other bottleneck the manuscript names for graph isomorphism. DARC keeps the problem open until the claim is checked.

# Applications

Few direct ones. Faster isomorphism testing is used in computational group theory systems and in classifying small groups. The tensor-isomorphism consequences bear on algorithms for algebra and polynomial isomorphism, and on the security analysis of signature proposals based on tensor-isomorphism-complete problems, which would need larger parameters if the reported $q^{O(n)}$ bound holds.

# Resolution criteria

For part (a): expert acceptance of a polynomial-time algorithm, through peer review, an independent re-derivation of the key steps, or a formal proof, ideally with an implementation that agrees with existing group-isomorphism software on benchmark families; if an error is found, the problem reverts to the state before November 2026. For part (b): matching upper and lower bounds, or a stated complexity-class membership, for some natural variant.

# Verification

Route: expert review of the 282-page manuscript. Reviewers should (1) restate the reduction from Cayley-table group isomorphism to extensions of elementary abelian groups by smaller groups, with the base case of groups without abelian normal subgroups taken from earlier work; (2) check the central step, the q^O(n) algorithm for 3-tensor isomorphism, in particular the matrix-group divide-and-conquer recursion with its progress measure, and the new characteristic-2 matrix tuple congruence algorithm; (3) confirm the running-time analysis gives poly(N). A cheap computational check, once code exists, is to implement the algorithm and compare it with the generator-enumeration algorithm on all groups of small order and on random class-2 p-groups. A formalization of the reduction steps would count as strong evidence.

# Plain explanation

Imagine two huge multiplication tables, each describing a system of things that can be combined. You want to know whether they describe the same system with the names shuffled. The straightforward method tries a very large number of matchings, and its running time grows far faster than any ordinary formula in the table's size.

Researchers have wanted a fast method for fifty years, partly because the question sits next to a famous unsolved one: telling whether two networks of dots and lines are the same.

On 3 November 2026, two researchers posted a 282-page paper to a public list of submissions for a theory conference. It reports a fast method, and says the main results were generated by ChatGPT during long conversations, then rewritten and taken responsibility for by the authors. Another group posted a short paper that uses the method for related objects.

Nobody outside the authors has reported checking the long argument. Papers like this take months to referee. Until experts examine it, the honest status is: claimed, unconfirmed. Even if it holds, it would not by itself settle the network question, and nobody knows how low the running time can go.

# Student explanation

**The question.** Given the Cayley tables of two groups of order $N$, decide whether they are isomorphic in time $N^{O(1)}$. Before November 2026 the best general bounds were $N^{\log_2 N+O(1)}$ by enumerating generator images (attributed to Tarjan, 1970s) and an improvement of the exponent by a constant factor (Rosenbaum, 2013).

**Why it is hard.** Special families are easy, so the hard core is $p$-groups. A $p$-group of class 2 and exponent $p$ is described by a skew-symmetric product $V\times V\to W$ between vector spaces over $\mathbb F_p$, which is a tensor. Isomorphism of such groups is exactly the question of whether two $n\times n\times n$ tensors over $\mathbb F_q$ differ by $(L,R,T)\in GL_n(\mathbb F_q)^3$, acting by multiplying the left, right and 'depth' directions. Brute force costs $q^{O(n^2)}$. Sun (2023) reached $q^{O(n^{1.8}\log q)}$ by fixing ('individualising') some slices and reducing to a matrix tuple congruence problem, and later work gave $q^{\tilde O(n^{1.5})}$. Each such bound translates to a group isomorphism bound because the group has order $N=q^{\Theta(n)}$ in the relevant case.

**What is claimed.** Qiao and Zhang (manuscript dated 3 November 2026, file 1723 in a GitHub release labelled as STOC 2027 submissions, unrefereed) report $q^{O(n)}$ time for tensors and $\mathrm{poly}(N)$ for all groups. They combine the Luks-Miyazaki matrix group divide-and-conquer framework with structure theory of linearly primitive groups and new linear-algebraic individualisation, and reach the general case through the solvable radical and group extensions classified by cohomology. The abstract says the main results were generated by ChatGPT in long conversations with the authors.

**Related claims.** Zhang, Wang and Zhuang (arXiv:2611.18084, 13 November 2026, 10 pages) build on it for rings and bilinear maps; this depends on the main result rather than checking it.

**What remains open.** Correctness has not been independently verified. Beyond that: the right exponent, whether $q^{o(n)}$ is possible for tensors (an 'ETH for tensor isomorphism', as the manuscript puts it), parallel complexity, and tournament isomorphism, the other bottleneck for putting graph isomorphism in P. The manuscript does not claim to settle graph isomorphism. DARC lists the problem as open until the claim is checked.
