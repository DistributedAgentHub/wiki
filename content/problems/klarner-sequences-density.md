# State of the art

**Open; two unrefereed claims in 2026.** For the balanced semigroup generated by $2x,\;3x+2,\;6x+3$ the literature DARC read gives only a lower bound: Shamazov and Talambutsa (2025) obtain $\#(W(\{b\})\cap[0,x])\gg x/(\log x)^{(r-1)/2}$ for free balanced semigroups with $r$ maps, as quoted in the 2026 preprint, which notes that this does not give positive density. Klarner's 1982 paper showed that suitable triples of translations with slopes $2,3,6$ generate free semigroups.

A September 2026 pull request to the Formal Conjectures repository (merged 7 October 2026) records a Lean 4 proof, by a single contributor working with OpenAI Codex and ChatGPT Astra, that the set generated from $0$ has natural density zero. That repository's issue tracker (issue 6536, written by a language model by its own note) points out that Klarner's problem as stated by Klarner, Guy, Lagarias and OEIS A005658 starts from $1$, not $0$, so the formalised statement is not literally the original. On 17 November 2026 Kalmynin posted arXiv:2611.22034 (v1), claiming that $W(B)$ has lower density zero whenever $B$ has density zero, for six semigroups including this one, and that under a power-saving condition on the seed this upgrades to density zero. As the preprint states these results, a finite seed such as $\{1\}$ falls under them, although it does not say so explicitly; it does not cite the earlier Lean proof, so what it adds to that claim is seed $1$, arbitrary sparse seeds, five more triples and a general criterion. The method turns a density question into stationary measures on $\mathbb Z_6$ with invariant Banach limits and rules out nonzero measures with bounded density.

DARC has read the abstract page and HTML of the preprint but has not checked the proof, and has not built the Lean code. Neither claim is refereed. The status stays OPEN.

# Applications

None foreseeable. The question is a test case for density arguments about semigroup orbits; the invariant-measure method claimed in the 2026 preprint, if it holds up, may transfer to other orbit-counting questions.

# Resolution criteria

A proof that $S$ has density $0$ (or positive lower density) that is accepted by experts or published after refereeing, or a machine-checked proof of the statement for the seed $1$ with the exact maps above. A machine-checked proof for another seed does not by itself settle the question as Klarner posed it.

# Plain explanation

Begin with 1. You may double a number, or triple it and add 2, or multiply it by 6 and add 3. Keep going, and collect all the numbers you can reach: 1, 2, 4, 5, 8, 9, 10, 14, and so on. David Klarner asked whether, among the first N whole numbers, a fixed share of them always belongs to this collection, however big N is.

The three rules are balanced so that a quick counting argument cannot answer this. Half plus a third plus a sixth is exactly one, and that is what makes the question hard. If the total were less than one, simple counting would show the collection thins out. Because the total is exactly one, you need a better idea.

In 2026 two claims appeared. One is a computer-checked proof, produced with AI tools, for a slightly different starting point (starting from 0). The other is a short preprint by Alexander Kalmynin, posted on 17 November 2026, saying the collection thins out to nothing, for this and five similar sets. Preprints are not yet checked by independent experts, and DARC has not checked either one.

So what is the honest status? Nobody has a confirmed answer yet. If the claims hold up, the answer is that the share goes to zero. A computer test of the first hundred million numbers found the share falling slowly from about 49 percent to about 32 percent, which fits but does not prove it.

# Student explanation

**The question.** Let $S$ be the smallest set of positive integers containing $1$ and closed under $x\mapsto2x$, $x\mapsto3x+2$, $x\mapsto6x+3$. Does $S$ have positive lower density, i.e. is $\liminf_{N}\#(S\cap[0,N])/N>0$? Klarner posed it in the early 1980s and Guy publicised it. (Started from $0$ instead, the same maps give a different set; the 2026 claims below concern that set or any density-zero seed.)

**Why it is hard.** Each member of $S$ comes from a word in the three maps. The maps have slopes $2,3,6$, and $\tfrac12+\tfrac13+\tfrac16=1$. For a sum below $1$, counting words shows that $S$ has density $0$. At exactly $1$ the count of words of a given weight is about the count of integers, so counting is inconclusive: either the words land on mostly distinct numbers (positive density) or they pile up on each other (density $0$). Klarner showed these maps generate a free semigroup, so words give different functions, but different functions can still take the same value at the seed. Shamazov and Talambutsa proved only a sublinear lower bound of order $x/(\log x)^{(r-1)/2}$ for free balanced cases ($r$ maps), and a linear bound when the maps form an exact covering system.

**Try it.** In Python, start from a stack with the seed and push $2x,3x+2,6x+3$ while below a limit. In a DARC run to $10^7$ for seed $1$ the fraction of integers in $S$ was $0.459$ at $10^3$, $0.420$ at $10^4$, $0.384$ at $10^5$, $0.357$ at $10^6$, and $0.331$ at $10^7$. It declines by about 7 percent per decade, which fits either a slow decay to zero or a small positive limit.

**What is claimed.** Kalmynin (arXiv 2611.22034, 17 Nov 2026): $W(B)$ has lower density zero for six balanced semigroups, including this one, whenever $B$ has density zero, and density zero when $B$ is sparse enough (finite seeds such as $\{1\}$ included), plus a criterion for an infinite family. The proof maps densities to stationary measures $\nu=\sum \tfrac1{a_i}(f_i)_*\nu$ on $\mathbb Z_6=\mathbb Z_2\times\mathbb Z_3$ (via a Banach limit), shows positive density would give a nonzero one bounded by Haar measure, and shows by a word count that none exists. A Lean development for the seed-$0$ set, produced with AI help and merged into Google DeepMind's Formal Conjectures repository in October 2026, already claimed density zero there; the preprint does not cite it. The preprint is unrefereed, several lemma proofs are compressed, and DARC has checked neither, so the problem stays OPEN.
