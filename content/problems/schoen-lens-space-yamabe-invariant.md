Let $p\ge2$ and let $L=S^3/\mathbb Z_p$ be a lens space, a quotient of the round 3-sphere by a free isometric action of the cyclic group $\mathbb Z_p$. The round metric descends to $L$ and gives a conformal class whose Yamabe constant is $Y(S^3)\,p^{-2/3}$, so $Y(L)\ge Y(S^3)\,p^{-2/3}$. **Conjecture (Schoen):** equality holds, $Y(L)=Y(S^3)/p^{2/3}$, i.e. the Yamabe invariant of every lens space is attained by the round metric. A full answer would also say whether the round conformal class is the only one that attains it.

# State of the art

**Open as of today.** The case $p=1$ is the solved Yamabe problem for $S^3$ (Aubin, Schoen), and the case $p=2$, $\mathbb{RP}^3$, was proved by Bray and Neves (Ann. of Math. 2004) using inverse mean curvature flow, in a classification of the prime 3-manifolds whose Yamabe invariant exceeds that of $\mathbb{RP}^3$. **A preprint claims** (Mazurowski, Wang, Yao and Zhou, arXiv:2611.08957, v1 of 6 November 2026) the conjecture for all lens spaces, with rigidity (equality only for the round conformal class). Its key step is that every $\mathbb Z_p$-invariant conformal class on $S^3$ with positive Yamabe constant has a $\mathbb Z_p$-equivariant Poincaré–Einstein filling, whose distance function gives an invariant test function with Yamabe quotient at most $Y(S^3)$. The abstract states that the proof of the main theorem was generated by AI (ChatGPT Astra, September 2026) and that the paper is mainly a learning note on that proof. It is unrefereed, and DARC found no independent assessment yet, so the problem stays OPEN.

# Applications

None foreseeable. The result would complete the computation of the Yamabe invariant for one family of 3-manifolds, which is part of classifying 3-manifolds by the scalar curvature they can carry. The Poincaré–Einstein filling method might be reused for other conformal-geometry questions.

# Resolution criteria

A proof accepted by experts in geometric analysis, ideally published in a refereed journal, that $Y(S^3/\mathbb Z_p)=Y(S^3)\,p^{-2/3}$ for all $p$ (or a counterexample lens space with a larger Yamabe invariant). An arXiv preprint, including an AI-generated proof, does not by itself count as a resolution.

# Plain explanation

Take a round ball-like space in three dimensions, the 3-sphere. Now imagine gluing it to itself using a repeating symmetry, such as a rotation that you repeat p times before returning to the start. The result is a new three-dimensional shape called a lens space.

Every shape has a number that says how evenly and strongly it can be curved, called the Yamabe invariant. A natural guess, made by the mathematician Richard Schoen, is that for a lens space the best option is the simple one: the round shape inherited from the sphere, which gives the sphere's number divided by a factor that depends on p. For the simplest lens space, real projective space, this guess was proved in 2004 by Hubert Bray and Andre Neves. For the others it was open.

In November 2026 four mathematicians posted a paper that claims it is true for all lens spaces. Its authors say that the main proof was written by an AI system, ChatGPT Astra, in September 2026, and that their paper is mainly a set of study notes on it. Nobody independent has checked it yet, so the question stays open here until experts do.

Why is it hard? The proof has to work for every possible way of curving the shape, not just the round one, and there are infinitely many.

The gluing picture has a limit: a real three-dimensional space cannot be drawn on paper.
