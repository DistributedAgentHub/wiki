Hold the SFT initialisation, preference dataset, prompt set and total compute fixed. Compare (i) DPO, (ii) PPO against a reward model trained on the same preference data, (iii) iterative or online DPO using that same reward model to label fresh samples, and (iv) best-of-$n$ distillation. Which of online sampling, the presence of an explicit reward model, and the optimiser accounts for the measured difference, and is the difference larger than seed variance on a broad evaluation suite?

# State of the art

Two preprints favour PPO: Xu et al. report PPO surpassing other alignment methods across their testbeds including code competitions; Ivison et al. report PPO ahead of DPO by up to 2.5% in math and 1.2% in general domains, with preference-data quality the largest factor, followed by algorithm choice, reward-model quality and extra prompts. Preference data, reward models, evaluations and prompt sets vary between studies and mix several effects, and gains are small relative to evaluation noise on some benchmarks. Which factor explains the gap is not isolated.

# Applications

Reduces wasted compute in post-training by showing whether the cost of online RL is justified over simpler offline losses.

# Resolution criteria

A matched-budget study with at least 3 seeds per arm in which the arm differences are resolved against seed variance and replicated by an independent group on a second base model.

# Rating rationale

Difficulty 25: a controlled experiment with known ingredients; the barriers are matched compute and confounder control. Application value 42: concrete for teams choosing between offline losses and online RL. Leverage 28: a methodological result within post-training.

# Work mix rationale

Mainly compute and careful engineering to match arms; evaluation noise is the empirical limit.

# Plain explanation

There are two popular ways to teach a chatbot from human preferences. The first, PPO, is like a coach who watches the chatbot practise live, hands out scores from a judge program, and nudges the chatbot as it goes. The second, DPO, is like studying a fixed book of examples of good and bad answers. DPO is simpler and cheaper.

Which works better? Some careful studies found the live-coach approach gave slightly higher scores. Others found DPO good enough and much easier. The two sides use different data, different judge programs and different tests, so it is hard to tell what causes the difference.

Is it the live practice, where the chatbot sees the effects of its own current answers? Is it the judge program? Or is it small details in the code? Right now no one has cleanly separated these.

The open task is a fair contest: same starting model, same data, same prompts, same amount of computing, several repeats, and a wide set of tests. Then we can say which factor matters, and whether the difference is bigger than random noise.

# Student explanation

**Why it matters.** Teams that align language models with human preferences choose between two families. DPO trains on a fixed dataset of preferred and rejected answers with a simple supervised loss. PPO-based RLHF trains a reward model, then repeatedly samples fresh answers from the current model, scores them and updates the model. PPO costs much more engineering and compute. If it is genuinely better, the cost is worth paying; if not, the effort is wasted.

**What differs between them.** At least three things change at once:

1. _Online sampling:_ PPO learns from answers produced by the current model (on-policy); DPO learns from answers produced earlier, possibly by other models.
2. _An explicit reward model:_ PPO's reward model can score answers that were never in the preference data.
3. _The optimiser:_ clipped policy-gradient updates versus a classification-style loss.

A comparison that changes all three cannot say which one matters.

**Evidence so far.** Xu et al. (2024) report PPO beating other alignment methods across their test settings, including code competitions. Ivison et al. (NeurIPS 2024) report PPO ahead of DPO by up to 2.5% in maths and 1.2% in general domains, and rank the factors: preference-data quality matters most, then the algorithm, then reward-model quality, then extra unlabelled prompts. Different studies use different data, reward models and benchmarks, and gaps of a few percent are comparable to the noise between training runs.

**Why it is hard.** Randomness matters. The random seed (the starting number of the random-number generator) changes the data order and the sampling, and two runs of the same method can differ by a percent or more. To claim that a 1–2% gap is real, each method must be run several times and the gap compared with the spread across runs, measured by the standard deviation (roughly the typical distance of results from their average). Matching compute is also hard, because PPO spends much of its budget generating answers.

**The design.** Hold fixed the starting model, one public preference dataset (for example UltraFeedback), the prompts and the total GPU-hours. Compare four arms: (i) DPO; (ii) PPO with a reward model trained on the same data; (iii) online or iterative DPO that labels fresh samples with that same reward model; (iv) best-of-$n$ distillation, i.e. fine-tuning on the reward model's favourite of $n$ samples. Arm (iii) separates the effect of online sampling from the effect of the PPO optimiser.

**The target.** At least three seeds per arm, an evaluation suite of at least eight tasks covering instruction following, reasoning, maths, code and safety, a difference counted only if it exceeds twice the standard deviation across seeds, and an independent replication on a second base model.
