  body: "## What it is\n\nClassical Fourier analysis tests whether a function $f$ on an abelian group $Z$ is correlated with a character, a pure wave such as $x\\mapsto e^{2\\pi i \\xi x}$. For some questions waves are not enough. Counting three-term arithmetic progressions in a set is controlled by Fourier coefficients, but counting four-term progressions is not: a *quadratic* phase such as $e^{2\\pi i \\alpha x^2}$ can fool every Fourier test and still bias the count. **Higher-order Fourier analysis** replaces waves by a richer class of structured functions and asks when a function has none of them in it.\n\n## Gowers norms\n\nFor a bounded function $f$ on a compact abelian group $Z$ with normalized Haar measure, the Gowers $U^d$-norm averages the product of $2^d$ shifted copies of $f$ (some complex-conjugated) over a $d$-dimensional cube of shifts. Gowers introduced these norms in his 2001 proof of Szemerédi's theorem. A small $U^{k+1}$-norm means $f$ is *uniform of order $k$*: averages of $f$ over linear configurations of length about $k+2$ behave as if $f$ were random. A large norm means some structure is present.\n\n## Inverse theorems\n\nAn **inverse theorem** says what that structure is: if $\\|f\\|_{U^{k+1}}\\ge\\delta$ and $f$ is 1-bounded, then $f$ correlates, with a bound depending only on $k$ and $\\delta$, with a *structured function* from an explicit class. The class consists of **nilsequences**: take a nilmanifold $G/\\Gamma$ (a nilpotent Lie group modulo a lattice), a morphism $\\phi$ from the group into it (a polynomial map), and a Lipschitz function $F$ on $G/\\Gamma$, and use $F\\circ\\phi$. Known cases listed in the Candela–González-Sánchez–Szegedy introduction: Green–Tao–Ziegler for intervals of integers and cyclic groups, Bergelson–Tao–Ziegler and Tao–Ziegler for vector spaces over finite fields, and the general nilspace inverse theorem of Candela–Szegedy for compact abelian groups. Nilsequences are necessary: polynomial phases alone do not suffice for the integer case.\n\n## Nilspaces\n\nA **nilspace** (Antolín Camarena–Szegedy; building on Host–Kra parallelepiped structures) is a set with a family of cubes obeying composition, ergodicity and corner-completion axioms; it generalizes the cubes of an abelian group. A $k$-step nilspace has unique completion of $(k+1)$-corners. Compact finite-rank nilspaces are the structures that appear in the general inverse theorem; when their structure groups are all tori (toral nilspaces) they are isomorphic to filtered nilmanifolds.\n\n## The open front\n\nThe general inverse theorem gives compact finite-rank nilspaces, which are more general than nilmanifolds. Jamneshan and Tao conjectured that, for finite abelian groups, nilmanifolds suffice. Special cases (bounded torsion, bounded rank) were obtained between 2023 and 2026, the same authors say they had an unpublished proof for the $U^4$ norm, and a 12 November 2026 preprint by Candela, González-Sánchez and Szegedy claims the general case. Quantitative versions, with effective bounds, are a separate and active direction."
  student: "**The question.** Take a set of whole numbers and ask how many arithmetic progressions it contains, such as 3, 7, 11 (equally spaced numbers). If the set were random with density $\\delta$, we would expect a fixed fraction of all progressions to land inside it. The tool for counting them is an average of the form $\\mathbb E_{x,d} f(x)f(x+d)f(x+2d)$, where $f$ is $1$ on the set and $0$ off it, and the average is over all starting points $x$ and step sizes $d$. The quantity is near its random value when $f$ has no structure of the right kind.\n\n**Waves are enough for three-term progressions.** Fourier analysis writes $f$ as a sum of waves, $x\\mapsto e^{2\\pi i\\xi x}$. For the count of three-term progressions, only the presence of a wave matters: if no wave correlates with $f$, the count is close to its random value. For four-term progressions, a wave test is not enough, because a *quadratic* phase such as $e^{2\\pi i\\alpha x^2}$ is invisible to every wave test and still shifts the count.\n\n**Gowers norms.** For each $k$ there is a score, the Gowers $U^{k+1}$-norm, that is small when $f$ is free of structure of polynomial degree up to $k$. It is an average of products of $2^{k+1}$ shifted copies of $f$ over a cube of shifts. Gowers introduced these norms to prove Szemerédi's theorem: a dense set of integers contains progressions of every length.\n\n**Inverse theorems.** The inverse question: if the score is not small, what is the structure? The answer is a **nilsequence**: pick a *nilmanifold* (a curved space built from a group whose commutators vanish after a few steps, folded up like a torus), start at a point, move along by a polynomial rule, and read off a continuous function. In symbols, for a 1-bounded $f$ on a compact abelian group $Z$ with large norm, there is a nilmanifold $G/\\Gamma$, a polynomial map $\\phi$ from $Z$ into it and a Lipschitz function $F$ on it such that $|\\mathbb E_{x\\in Z} f(x)F(\\phi(x))|\\ge\\varepsilon$. The right side is a fixed positive number that depends on the norm and the degree but not on the group $Z$. Such theorems exist for intervals of integers (Green–Tao–Ziegler) and for vector spaces over finite fields.\n\n**Nilspaces.** To get theorems for all abelian groups at once, Antolín Camarena and Szegedy introduced *nilspaces*: sets with a notion of cube, generalizing the cubes in $Z$ that appear in the norm. The general theorem (Candela–Szegedy) gives a morphism into a *compact finite-rank nilspace*, not necessarily a nilmanifold.\n\n**The open point.** Jamneshan and Tao conjectured that for finite abelian groups the target can always be a nilmanifold. A November 2026 preprint claims this is true; see the problem page."

# Plain explanation

Imagine two long lists of coins, heads or tails. One list was made by flipping a fair coin. The other was made by a rule, but a rule so tangled that it looks random at first glance. Can you tell them apart? For some questions you cannot just look: if you want to count how often three equally spaced spots in the list are all heads, a simple wave-like pattern in the list can give it away. For counting four or five equally spaced spots, patterns that are subtler than waves can matter.

Higher-order Fourier analysis builds a ruler for this. It gives each list a score. A low score says the list is random enough that counts of evenly spaced patterns behave as expected. A high score says some hidden pattern is there. Then a second kind of theorem, called an inverse theorem, says what the hidden pattern must look like: it must resemble a function that moves around on a special curved space.

This matters for questions such as how many evenly spaced numbers, like 3, 7, 11, 15, a large set of numbers must contain. It is pure mathematics; the use is as a tool for proving other theorems.

A limit of the picture: the lists here are functions on whole families of number systems at once, and the hidden patterns are exact objects, not a vague sense of order.
