## What it is

The cosmic microwave background (CMB) is the thermal radiation that was released when the universe cooled enough, roughly 380,000 years after the Big Bang, for electrons and nuclei to combine into neutral atoms and for photons to travel freely. Stretched by the expansion, it now arrives as microwaves. Its spectrum is the most perfect blackbody measured in nature: the FIRAS instrument on COBE found a maximum deviation from a blackbody below 0.03% over 0.5 to 5 mm, and the final analyses give a temperature of $2.725 \pm 0.002$ K (Mather et al. 1999) and 95% limits on spectral distortions of $|\mu| < 9\times10^{-5}$ and $|y| < 1.5\times10^{-5}$ (Fixsen et al. 1996). That agreement is itself a strong constraint: any energy injected into the plasma at later times would have distorted it.

## Anisotropies

The temperature is almost uniform, with variations of about one part in $10^5$. These ripples are sound waves in the photon-baryon plasma, frozen at recombination. The positions and heights of the acoustic peaks encode the geometry of space, the baryon and dark-matter densities, the expansion history and the amplitude and tilt of the primordial fluctuations. Planck constrained 18 peaks in the temperature and polarization spectra, measured five of the six base ΛCDM parameters to better than 1%, and fixed the angular size of the sound horizon, $\theta_*$, to 0.03%.

## Polarization and lensing

Thomson scattering of a quadrupolar radiation field polarizes the CMB. The pattern is decomposed into gradient-like E modes and curl-like B modes. Scalar density fluctuations make only E modes at recombination, while a background of primordial gravitational waves from inflation would also make B modes. Gravitational lensing by intervening matter converts some E into B and so produces a guaranteed B-mode signal (detected at 7.0 sigma in the 2015 joint BICEP2/Keck Array and Planck analysis), which is now a limiting contaminant for primordial searches and is removed by "delensing". Polarized Galactic dust is the other main foreground; the 2014 BICEP2 claim of a primordial detection was later shown to be consistent with dust.

## Where things stand (October 2026)

The ground-based Atacama Cosmology Telescope (ACT, DR6) and South Pole Telescope (SPT-3G) now match or exceed Planck on small scales. The CMB-only Planck+ACT combination (P-ACT) gives $H_0 = 67.62 \pm 0.50$ km/s/Mpc and a spectral index $n_s = 0.9709 \pm 0.0038$; adding CMB lensing and DESI DR1 BAO (P-ACT-LB) gives $H_0 = 68.22 \pm 0.36$ and $n_s = 0.974 \pm 0.003$. Both are higher than Planck 2018 alone ($n_s = 0.9649 \pm 0.0042$), a shift that matters for which inflation models are favoured, although the P-ACT-LB value also includes BAO and is not a like-for-like comparison. SPT-3G alone (temperature, polarization and lensing) gives $H_0 = 66.66 \pm 0.60$, and the combination of SPT, ACT and Planck (CMB-SPA) gives $H_0 = 67.19 \pm 0.38$, $\sigma_8 = 0.8137 \pm 0.0037$ and $n_s = 0.9679 \pm 0.0033$. The SPT-3G authors conclude that CMB data alone show no clear sign of physics beyond ΛCDM, while noting a 2.8 sigma difference between CMB and DESI DR2 BAO within ΛCDM. The CMB-derived $H_0$ is one side of the Hubble tension; the CMB-derived clustering amplitude is the baseline for the S8 comparison.

## What is next

The main target is the tensor-to-scalar ratio $r$. The best published limit is BICEP/Keck's $r < 0.036$ (95%), or $r < 0.032$ when combined with Planck and BAO. Planned improvements are the Simons Observatory's expanded small-aperture array (forecast $\sigma(r) \approx 1.2\times10^{-3}$ with 70% delensing), the South Pole Observatory combination of BICEP Array with the SPT-3G+ receiver (installation planned for early 2029, goal $\sigma(r) = 0.001$; both forecasts are from preprints), and the JAXA-led LiteBIRD satellite, whose design target is $\delta r = 0.001$ and whose launch JAXA now aims for in Japan's fiscal year 2036, after a 2025 reformulation of the mission. The US DOE and NSF ended support for the CMB-S4 project in July 2025, which removed the previously planned flagship ground-based effort.

## Method note

Theory predictions come from linearised Einstein-Boltzmann codes (for example CAMB and CLASS); analyses combine many frequency maps, remove foregrounds and fit likelihoods over millions of data points. The signal is so well modelled that a surprise in the data is more likely to be a systematic than new physics, which is why independent experiments matter.

# Plain explanation

In its first few hundred thousand years the universe was a hot glowing fog. Light could not travel far because it kept bumping into free electrons. As the universe expanded, it cooled. At about 380,000 years old, the fog cleared and the light flew free. That light has been travelling ever since. Space has stretched during the trip, which has turned the light from visible glow into faint microwaves, colder than anywhere you will ever visit: about 2.7 degrees above absolute zero.

This is the cosmic microwave background, or CMB. It comes from every direction. It was found by accident in 1965 by Arno Penzias and Robert Wilson, two Bell Labs radio astronomers who could not get rid of a hiss in their antenna.

The glow is almost perfectly even, but not quite. There are slightly warmer and cooler patches, different by about one part in 100,000. These patches are a snapshot of the sound waves that were rippling through the early universe's fog. Their sizes and patterns tell us how much ordinary matter, dark matter and dark energy exist, how old the universe is, and what shape space has.

The light is also polarized, meaning it has a preferred direction of wiggle. Nobody yet knows whether that wiggle hides a faint swirl left by gravitational waves from the universe's first instant.

Where the analogy breaks: a fog in the sky would clear from the outside in, but the CMB comes from everywhere at once, like a sphere around us.

# Student explanation

**Origin.** At early times the universe was a hot plasma of photons, electrons and nuclei. Photons scattered off free electrons (Thomson scattering) so often that they could not travel freely. When the temperature fell to about 3000 K, roughly 380,000 years after the Big Bang, electrons and nuclei combined into neutral atoms (recombination) and the photons were free to stream. We see these photons from a spherical shell called the surface of last scattering. The expansion since then has stretched their wavelength by a factor of about 1100.

**A perfect blackbody.** The FIRAS instrument on the COBE satellite measured the spectrum and found it matches a blackbody to better than 0.03%, at a temperature of 2.725 K. Any energy added to the plasma at later times would have distorted the spectrum, so the close match gives tight limits on such events.

**Anisotropies.** The temperature varies by about one part in 10⁵ across the sky. These are sound waves in the photon–baryon plasma, frozen at recombination. We analyze the map by breaking it into spherical-harmonic multipoles and plotting the power spectrum against multipole ℓ (small ℓ is large angular scales). The spectrum has a series of acoustic peaks. Their positions give the geometry of space (combined with other data, space is flat to within a fraction of a per cent). Their relative heights give the baryon and dark-matter densities. The Planck satellite measured these peaks and fixed five of the six ΛCDM parameters to better than 1%.

**Polarization.** Scattering of light that has a quadrupole pattern produces linear polarization. Polarization maps split into E modes (gradient-like) and B modes (curl-like). Ordinary density ripples make only E modes at recombination. Primordial gravitational waves from inflation would also make B modes. The ratio of gravitational-wave to density amplitude is called the tensor-to-scalar ratio r; the present limit is around r < 0.03 to 0.04.

**Lensing and foregrounds.** On its way to us, the light is bent by the gravity of matter in between (gravitational lensing), which converts some E modes into B modes and which also lets us map matter. Dust and synchrotron emission from our own galaxy also produce polarized foregrounds that need to be removed.

**Why it matters.** The CMB is the cleanest probe of the early universe and the main evidence behind the standard model.
