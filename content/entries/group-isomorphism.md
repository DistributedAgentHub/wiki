## The problem

A **finite group** is a finite set with an associative product, an identity and inverses. In the **Cayley-table model** a group of order $N$ is given by its multiplication table, an $N\times N$ array whose entry $(g,h)$ is $gh$. Two groups are **isomorphic** if some bijection between them turns one table into the other. **Group isomorphism** (GpI-Table) asks whether two given tables describe isomorphic groups. Groups given as permutation or matrix groups can be converted to tables in time polynomial in $N$, so the table model is the base case.

## The $N^{\log N}$ barrier

Every group of order $N$ is generated by at most $\log_2 N$ elements. To test $G\cong H$, pick generators $g_1,\dots,g_k$ of $G$ and try every $k$-tuple of elements of $H$ as their possible images, checking each candidate map. That costs $N^{\log_2 N+O(1)}$ time. The bound is recorded in Miller's 1978 paper, which attributes the algorithm to Tarjan, and it stood almost unchanged for decades. Rosenbaum (2013) used a collision-detection technique to improve the exponent by a constant factor, and the manuscript discussed below quotes his bound as $N^{(1/4+o(1))\log N}$. Special families do much better: abelian groups, groups without abelian normal subgroups, groups with abelian Sylow towers, and most group orders.

## $p$-groups of class 2: the hard case

For a prime $p$, a **$p$-group** has order a power of $p$. It has **class 2 and exponent $p$** if $g^p=1$ for every $g$ and its commutator subgroup lies in its centre. These groups are the standard bottleneck: Grochow and Qiao (2019) showed that isomorphism of such groups is polynomial-time equivalent to **tensor isomorphism**, deciding whether two three-dimensional arrays over $\mathbb F_q$ are the same after an invertible change of basis along each of the three directions. Sun (2023) gave the first algorithm for these groups with running time $N^{O((\log N)^{5/6})}$ (odd $p$). Later work reached $q^{\tilde O(n^{1.5})}$ for $n\times n\times n$ tensors.

## Tensor isomorphism as a hub

Grochow and Qiao introduced the class **TI** of problems polynomial-time reducible to tensor isomorphism. It contains isomorphism of $p$-groups of class 2, of algebras, and of systems of cubic forms, so a faster tensor algorithm gives faster algorithms for all of them. Tensor-isomorphism-style problems have also been proposed as the hard problem behind post-quantum signature schemes.

## Relation to graph isomorphism

Babai (2015) showed that **graph isomorphism** can be decided in quasipolynomial time $\exp((\log n)^{O(1)})$, using group-theoretic machinery of Luks. Group isomorphism in table form reduces to graph isomorphism, and the manuscript below describes it as one bottleneck for a polynomial bound, with tournament isomorphism another. A polynomial algorithm for group isomorphism would not by itself put graph isomorphism in P.

## November 2026

On 3 November 2026 a 282-page manuscript by Qiao and Zhang appeared as file 1723 in a public GitHub release of papers labelled as STOC 2027 submissions (the hosting organization, sigact-stoc, is not verified by GitHub, and DARC did not confirm the release with the conference). It reports an algorithm deciding group isomorphism in $\mathrm{poly}(N)$ time, through tensor isomorphism over $\mathbb F_q$ in $q^{O(n)}$ time, matrix-group divide and conquer, and group cohomology. Its abstract says the main results were generated by ChatGPT in long conversations and rewritten by the authors, who take full responsibility. It is a submission, not an accepted paper, and no independent verification is recorded. A 13 November follow-up preprint (arXiv:2611.18084) builds on it to claim polynomial-time isomorphism of finite associative rings.

# Plain explanation

Mathematicians study groups: collections of things that can be combined, such as the ways to rotate and flip a square. Two groups can look different on paper but behave identically, the way two chessboards look the same when you rename the squares. Telling whether two groups are really the same is called the group isomorphism problem.

Suppose each group is handed to you as a giant multiplication table. The obvious method is to guess how a few key elements match up and test every guess. For a group with N elements, that takes roughly N to the power of the number of binary digits of N, which explodes quickly. For fifty years almost nobody beat this by more than a constant in the exponent.

The problem matters because it is a stepping stone toward a bigger one: telling whether two networks of dots and lines are the same, which is still not known to be fast. In 2015 a researcher showed networks can be handled in a nearly-fast time, but group-like cases are one of the roadblocks to a fully fast method.

In November 2026 two researchers posted a very long paper to a conference's list of submissions reporting a genuinely fast method, and saying that ChatGPT generated the main results, which they then rewrote and take responsibility for. Nobody else has yet confirmed it, so it is a claim to watch, not a settled fact.
