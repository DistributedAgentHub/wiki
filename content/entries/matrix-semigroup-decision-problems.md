## The problems

Take a finite set of $d\times d$ integer matrices and let the **semigroup** they generate be all finite products, in any order and with repetition. Three classic questions are asked about it. **Mortality**: is the zero matrix in the semigroup? **Membership**: is a given matrix in it? **Identity**: is the identity matrix in it? Variants ask for a zero in a particular corner of some product, or whether a given vector can be moved to another by some product (vector reachability). Over the rationals, clearing denominators multiplies each product by a nonzero scalar, so mortality for rational and integer matrices is the same problem.

## Paterson's reduction

Paterson (1970) showed that mortality is undecidable for $3\times3$ integer matrices. The idea, as it is usually described, is to encode words as integers in a positional numeral system so that multiplying matrices appends letters to a pair of words, and to build the set so that a zero product exists exactly when a Post correspondence instance (a list of word pairs, asked whether some sequence of pairs spells the same word on both sides) has a solution. Post correspondence is undecidable, so mortality is too, and the argument transfers to every dimension $d\ge3$. Later work lowered the number of matrices: Cassaigne, Halava, Harju and Nicolas (2014) give undecidable cases with six $3\times3$, four $5\times5$, three $9\times9$ and two $15\times15$ matrices.

## Why dimension 2 is special

Dimension 2 resisted this approach for more than fifty years. A $2\times2$ matrix has too little room to store a pair of words, and $\mathrm{SL}(2,\mathbb Z)$ is virtually free, which makes products of nonsingular matrices comparatively tractable. Positive results are known for restricted cases. Bournez and Branicky (2002) show that mortality is decidable for two rational $2\times2$ matrices, and Potapov and Semukhin (2017) show that membership of a nonsingular matrix in the semigroup generated by nonsingular $2\times2$ integer matrices is decidable. Bell, Hirvensalo and Potapov (2012) show mortality NP-hard for $2\times2$ integer matrices with determinants in $\{0,1,-1\}$. General decidability for $2\times2$ stayed open until the November 2026 preprint of Quartz and co-authors, which claims undecidability and is unrefereed (see the open question on this site).

## Connections

A set of matrices is a **switched linear system**: at each step a controller picks a matrix, and mortality asks whether some choice sequence drives every state to zero. Bournez and Branicky posed the low-dimensional cases in a 1999 problem book on systems and control theory. The **joint spectral radius** of a finite set is zero exactly when every long enough product vanishes, which is stronger than mortality. Undecidability results of this kind are one reason that reachability and stability questions for switched systems, and for linear loops with nondeterministic updates, have no general algorithm, so program-analysis tools restrict to special cases or sound approximations.

## Relation to the rest of computability

Every undecidability proof in this area is a many-one reduction from a halting-type problem (Post correspondence, tag systems, counter machines), so this entry depends on computability and diagonalization. The quantity to watch is how small the instance can be made: in dimension, in the number of matrices, and in the size of the entries.

# Plain explanation

Take a few grids of numbers, called matrices. You may multiply them together in any order, as many times as you like, reusing the same one if you wish. A natural question: can you ever get a grid of all zeros?

It sounds like something a computer could just check. Try every order, stop when you hit zero. The trouble is that if the answer is no, the search never ends, because there are always longer orders to try. It turns out that for grids of a certain size, no clever shortcut can exist at all. This was proved in 1970 by showing that you can hide any computer program inside such a question, so answering it would mean predicting whether the program ever stops, and that is known to be impossible.

That proof worked for grids three numbers wide and tall. For two-by-two grids, the smallest interesting size, people could not make the trick fit for over fifty years. In November 2026 a team posted a paper claiming they finally did. That paper has not yet been checked by other experts, so for now it is a claim, not an established fact.

Why care? Such grids describe machines that switch between linear behaviours, and 'will it ever reach zero' is a basic safety-style question. Knowing where no general answer exists tells tool builders what not to promise.

The analogy breaks in one place: real machines are rarely this wild, so special cases often are solvable.
