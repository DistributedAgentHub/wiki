  body: "## Sets generated by affine maps\n\nFix integer maps $f_i(x)=a_i x+b_i$ with slopes $a_i\\ge 2$, and a starting set $B$ (a seed). The **orbit set** $W(B)$ is the smallest set of integers that contains $B$ and is closed under every $f_i$; equivalently, all values of finite compositions of the maps applied to elements of $B$. These are the *Klarner–Rado sets*, named after the 1974 paper of Klarner and Rado on the arithmetic of such recursively defined sets. The basic question is how many elements the set has below $x$, and in particular whether it has positive (asymptotic) density.\n\n## Counting bounds and the balanced case\n\nIf $\\sum 1/a_i<1$, classical counting arguments (Erdős, and Klarner and Rado) give sublinear bounds, so the density is $0$. If the maps have equal slope $n$, that is $nx+b_i$, Klarner (1981) treated that case, and Konyagin, Protasov and Talambutsa (2025) treat the equal-slope case by refinement equations and Fourier analysis on $\\mathbb Z_n$. The hard regime is the **balanced** case $\\sum 1/a_i=1$ with mixed slopes, where counting arguments stop short of a decision.\n\nThe standard example has slopes $2,3,6$ ($\\tfrac12+\\tfrac13+\\tfrac16=1$) and maps $2x,\\;3x+2,\\;6x+3$. Klarner (1982) identified triples of translations for slopes $2,3,6$ that generate **free semigroups**, meaning that different compositions of the maps give different functions. Klarner's question, publicised by Guy, asks whether the set generated from the seed $1$ (OEIS A005658: 1, 2, 4, 5, 8, 9, 10, 14, ...) has positive lower density. The seed matters: the same maps started from $0$ give a different set ($0,2,3,4,6,8,11,\\dots$), and the Erdős-problems form of the question that a 2026 Lean formalization used starts from $0$ (a mismatch reported in a GitHub issue on that formalization).\n\n## What was known\n\n- Erdős proved an upper bound $O(x^{\\sigma+\\epsilon})$ for the counting function, with an exponent $\\sigma$ fixed by the slopes; Lagarias extended it to orbit multisets and real affine maps.\n- Shamazov and Talambutsa (arXiv 2025, revised 2026) proved a lower bound $\\Omega(x^{\\sigma})$ for orbit multisets, a sublinear lower bound for the orbit set when the maps form a basis of a free semigroup with $\\sum 1/a_i=1$ (of the form $x/(\\log x)^{(r-1)/2}$ for $r$ maps, as quoted in the 2026 preprint below), and a linear lower bound, hence positive density, when the maps form an exact covering system of the integers.\n- For the $2,3,6$ example, neither a proof of positive density nor a proof of density $0$ was in the literature DARC found before September 2026.\n\n## 2026 claims\n\nA Lean 4 development by a single contributor, produced with OpenAI Codex and ChatGPT Astra by its own README and merged into the Formal Conjectures repository in October 2026, claims that the seed-$0$ set has natural density zero. On 17 November 2026 Kalmynin posted an unrefereed preprint claiming density $0$ for this set and five further triples, by showing that no nonzero stationary measure with bounded density exists on the profinite group $\\mathbb Z_6=\\mathbb Z_2\\times\\mathbb Z_3$, plus a criterion covering an infinite family of mixed-slope semigroups. DARC has checked neither, and the question stays open here until a refereed or independently verified proof exists.\n\n## What is not known\n\nWhether the density is $0$ for balanced mixed-slope semigroups in general, how fast the counting function grows for the $2,3,6$ sets (a power saving is not claimed in the sources DARC read), and whether positive density can occur outside exact covering systems."

# Plain explanation

Start with the number 0. You are allowed three moves: double it, or triple it and add 2, or multiply it by 6 and add 3. Keep making new numbers from the ones you have. You get a set that begins 0, 2, 3, 4, 6, 8, 11, and so on. Question: if you look at the first million whole numbers, what share of them are in your set?

For a computer this is easy to try. In one test the share was about 49 percent among the first thousand numbers, then fell to about 32 percent among the first hundred million. It keeps dropping, but very slowly, so a table cannot tell whether it heads to zero or settles near some small number.

The moves are chosen so that, by a simple counting argument, the set could in principle fill a fixed share. Counting alone does not decide it. A mathematician named David Klarner asked this in the 1980s for the same rules started from 1, and Richard Guy helped make it known. A few related cases are solved. Other cases were open.

In 2026 a preprint (a paper not yet checked by other experts) claims the share goes to zero for this and five similar sets. An earlier computer-checked proof also claims this for one version of the question. Neither has been confirmed by independent referees yet.

An analogy: pouring water into a grid of buckets so that each bucket passes water on in fixed ratios. The question is whether the water spreads out so thin that no fixed share of buckets ever gets wet. Where the analogy breaks: here the buckets are numbers, and the passing rules are arithmetic.

# Student explanation

**Setup.** An **affine map** is a rule $f(x)=ax+b$ with whole numbers $a\ge2$ and $b$. Pick a few of them and a starting number (the **seed**), and let $S$ be the smallest set containing the seed and closed under all the rules. In Python: keep a stack, pop $x$, push $2x$, $3x+2$ and $6x+3$ if not seen yet and below a limit $N$. Count the members below $N$.

**Density.** The **natural density** of a set is the limit of $\#(S\cap[0,N))/N$ as $N\to\infty$, if it exists; the **lower density** uses the smallest long-run value (a $\liminf$, the least value that the ratio keeps coming back to). The question is whether the density is $0$ or positive.

**When counting settles it.** Each member of $S$ is built by a word in the maps. If the slopes $a_i$ satisfy $\sum 1/a_i<1$, then words of a given size produce few enough numbers that the set has density $0$, a counting argument going back to Erdős and to Klarner and Rado. If equal slopes are used, special methods apply. The delicate case is $\sum 1/a_i=1$, **balanced**, such as slopes $2,3,6$ with $\tfrac12+\tfrac13+\tfrac16=1$. In that case, if all the words give different numbers (the semigroup is **free**), the number of words of a given weight is about the same as the count of integers, and one cannot tell from counting whether the words overlap enough to thin the set. If the maps form an **exact covering system** (every integer is hit exactly once by the images), the set has positive density, and it was proved in 2025 (Shamazov and Talambutsa) that this happens. For free balanced semigroups only a sublinear lower bound of order $x/(\log x)^{(r-1)/2}$ was known.

**The 2026 method.** To work with $\mathbb N$ one passes to a compact group in which the maps still act: the profinite integers $\mathbb Z_6=\mathbb Z_2\times\mathbb Z_3$ (every number is described by all its remainders mod powers of 2 and 3). A **Banach limit** is a way of averaging a bounded sequence that is shift invariant; applying one to the indicator of $S\cap[0,N)$ turns positive lower density into a nonzero measure $\nu$ on $\mathbb Z_6$. The balance condition makes that measure **stationary**, $\nu=\sum_i \tfrac1{a_i}(f_i)_*\nu$, and positive density makes it bounded by the Haar measure. The preprint then proves that no such measure exists, using counts of words with good divisibility and the orbits of a few rational points, such as $-1/3\to-2/3\to0$.

**Status.** Kalmynin's preprint (17 Nov 2026) claims density $0$ for six triples and a criterion for an infinite family. A Lean development for one seed also claims it. DARC has checked none of it, and the question is listed as open.
